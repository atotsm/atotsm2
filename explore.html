04 · REIMAGINEMonetary and social alternativesExplore voluntary monetary pluralism, currency democracy, commons and cooperative governance.Unfold the territory ＋

Plurality rather than another monopoly

Currency democracy asks how people can meaningfully choose and combine monetary arrangements. The aim is not to replace a national currency with another single currency, nor to impose one alternative monetary philosophy. It is to enable a diverse ecology of exchange systems that can complement one another and serve different human purposes.

We already know more currencies than we think ＋

Airline miles, supermarket and retail points, festival tokens, gift cards, store credit and videogame currencies are already familiar parts of everyday life. Time credits, local currencies and mutual-credit networks extend the idea into care, community and economic cooperation. These systems are not identical, and not all are currencies in the same technical or legal sense. Yet they show that people already understand exchange systems designed around particular purposes.

In an Ikigai-oriented community, play, creativity, learning and shared experiences are meaningful dimensions of life. A game currency can therefore have a complementary role: it may recognise participation, collaboration or creative contribution, and could connect with other community systems where people choose to make those exchanges possible. Even when it remains inside a game, it can complement wider life by serving a distinct purpose. Convertibility is one possible design feature, not a prerequisite for complementary value.

The question is not whether people can imagine these designs. We already encounter many of them. The deeper question is how to build sufficient understanding, confidence and trustworthy conditions to use a wider range of models.

Two examples: mobility and care ＋

Curitiba, Brazil — bus transfer tokens. Curitiba's public transport system used paper transfer tokens to let passengers change buses without paying a second fare. The token served a specific purpose within a larger transport network. The paper-token arrangement was abandoned after widespread forgery, illustrating that useful exchange designs also need reliable verification and administration.

Japan — Fureai Kippu. These time-credit arrangements recognised help given to older people and others needing care. Participants could earn credits through assistance and, in some schemes, save or transfer them to obtain care later or support family members. The model made time, attention and reciprocity visible as forms of value that ordinary money may not adequately recognise.

How can trust grow? ＋

People need to understand how a system works, who stands behind it, what gives its credits value, how they can earn and use them, what happens when something goes wrong, and whether its rules are fair. Trust grows through transparent and understandable rules, accountable governance, reliable records, accessible ways to use or redeem credits, safeguards against abuse and real-world experience.

Start with a purpose, design with the people who will use the system, test it at a manageable scale, publish what works and what does not, and improve it together. The aim is not to persuade everyone to adopt every model. It is to make different possibilities understandable and trustworthy enough that communities can choose what genuinely serves them.

A note on Yin–Yang currencies ＋

In Bernard Lietaer's Yin–Yang framing, Yang-like currencies tend towards centralised issuance, competition and wider-scale exchange, while Yin-like currencies tend towards decentralised creation, cooperation and community relationships. These are tendencies, not moral labels. The aspiration is not to replace Yang with Yin, or to install another single currency, but to let different forms coexist and complement one another.

A monetary system in balance is not one currency doing everything. It is different currencies working together, each serving purposes for which it is suited, within fair and trustworthy arrangements.

  • National, local, mutual-credit, time-based, game-based and purpose currencies
  • Currency democracy and voluntary monetary pluralism
  • Commons, cooperatives, community governance and digital infrastructure
  • Plural systems designed around real needs, not tokens for their own sake

Three ways to meet

Different invitations. One human conversation.

People may approach the inquiry in different ways. These are modes of encounter, not professional personas or identities anyone has to adopt.

01 · THE AUDIT

Investigate together

For people and groups interested in systemic questions, evidence, policy, community design and the relationship between monetary arrangements and lived outcomes.

02 · THE HEARTH

Meet through everyday life

For walks, gardens, thoughtful conversations and gift-based connection—meeting as people, not reducing one another to professional or client roles.

03 · THE OPEN ENCOUNTER

Begin without a script

For an unprescribed exchange: curiosity, integrity and the possibility of an interesting meeting without a predetermined task or expected outcome.

A principle for the whole website

Depth when curiosity asks for it.

Start with a clear snapshot. Reveal definitions, examples, sources, methods and project specifics when a visitor chooses to explore further. Essential context and qualifications should remain visible; detail should be available without becoming an obstacle.

Respectful disclosure. Present the smallest useful amount of information first. Reveal greater depth, structure and substantiation when curiosity invites it. Keep every layer navigable, accessible and connected to the wider map.
