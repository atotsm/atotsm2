Usurology is a proposed interdisciplinary inquiry into how the structures of a society shape the possibilities available to people and communities — and how those structures might be understood, challenged and redesigned.

Working definition

The study of the conditions that organise life.

It asks how rules, institutions, ownership, monetary arrangements, technology, energy, culture and ecology interact. It is interested in how societies distribute time, risk, opportunity, dependency and agency.

A central research thread

Debt-based money deserves investigation — not immunity from criticism.

The original project placed monetary architecture near the centre of its diagnosis. That remains an important research thread, but a stronger version asks precise questions about mechanisms and conditions rather than assuming one cause explains every outcome.

Questions worth investigating

  • How do debt contracts and interest obligations distribute risk and income?
  • Under what conditions does debt support productive cooperation?
  • When do debt burdens constrain household or public choices?
  • How do monetary arrangements interact with energy, land, technology and political power?

What must not be assumed

  • That all interest-bearing credit is inherently extractive.
  • That debt alone explains inequality, burnout or ecological damage.
  • That money supply growth maps directly onto resource extraction.
  • That one alternative currency design solves every coordination problem.
Research posture: treat monetary architecture as a potentially powerful causal factor and amplifier. Compare mechanisms, seek counterexamples and separate accounting identities from behavioural claims.
A practical instrument

The ledger audit.

A ledger audit makes dependencies and flows visible. It is not a clinical diagnosis or proof of systemic causation; it is a structured inquiry into a person's or community's time, money, energy and obligations.

FlowQuestionsPossible evidence
MoneyWhere does income come from? What recurring obligations, interest costs and fees shape choices?Budgets, contracts, statements and prices.
TimeWhich hours are chosen, required, unpaid or spent maintaining systems?Time diaries, schedules and care responsibilities.
EnergyWhich activities restore capacity and which consume it?Workload, self-reports and environmental context.
AgencyWhere can a person negotiate, refuse, exit or influence the rules?Decision rights, contract terms and available options.
RelationshipsWhere is there reciprocity, trust, dependency or pressure?Interviews and voluntary network mapping.
The connection

Diagnosis without a picture of flourishing is incomplete.

Usurology can reveal constraints, but it cannot alone define what makes a life good. That requires the complementary inquiry of Ikigaiology — and the voices of the people affected.
