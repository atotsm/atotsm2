Lens one · Systems and conditions

beneath the surface.

Usurology is a proposed interdisciplinary inquiry into how the structures of a society shape the possibilities available to people and communities — and how those structures might be understood, challenged and redesigned.

Working definition

The study of the conditions that organise life.

It asks how rules, institutions, ownership, monetary arrangements, technology, energy, culture and ecology interact. It is interested in how societies distribute time, risk, opportunity, dependency and agency.

A central research thread

Usury: an ancient warning, a modern blind spot.

ATOTSM’s position is that interest is not a neutral price: an interest-bearing loan creates an extraction claim and contributes to the concentration of wealth. The wider monetary system can make debt dependence appear ordinary, necessary, beneficial—even heaven-promising—while its cumulative pressures narrow the choices of more and more people. Usurology investigates this structure and the forces that keep it difficult to see.

Questions worth investigating

  • How was usury understood across the past 5,000 years?
  • How did that understanding become forgotten, displaced or reframed?
  • Why can interest first appear innocent, beneficial and full of promise?
  • Why can its effects eventually become the opposite for growing numbers of people?
  • How does denial persist—and why can it still appear convincing or powerful?

What keeps the pattern in place?

  • Who benefits from the prevailing monetary and debt arrangements?
  • How do institutions, incentives, education and dominant narratives normalise interest?
  • How do debt obligations shape bargaining power, ownership and access to necessities?
  • How can the system’s apparent strength depend on people not seeing its deeper costs?
Why can the effects be hard to prove in public? Costs are distributed across contracts, prices, taxes, asset values, institutions and time; records and categories often isolate each transaction from the wider pattern. Powerful beneficiaries can shape which questions are funded, taught, measured or treated as legitimate. This makes systemic analysis harder—not impossible. ATOTSM will trace mechanisms, historical records, distributional outcomes and counter-evidence, while stating clearly what the evidence establishes and what remains to be tested.
A practical instrument

The ledger audit.

A ledger audit makes dependencies and flows visible. It is not a clinical diagnosis or proof of systemic causation; it is a structured inquiry into a person's or community's time, money, energy and obligations.

FlowQuestionsPossible evidence
MoneyWhere does income come from? What recurring obligations, interest costs and fees shape choices?Budgets, contracts, statements and prices.
TimeWhich hours are chosen, required, unpaid or spent maintaining systems?Time diaries, schedules and care responsibilities.
EnergyWhich activities restore capacity and which consume it?Workload, self-reports and environmental context.
AgencyWhere can a person negotiate, refuse, exit or influence the rules?Decision rights, contract terms and available options.
RelationshipsWhere is there reciprocity, trust, dependency or pressure?Interviews and voluntary network mapping.
The connection

Diagnosis without a picture of flourishing is incomplete.

Usurology can reveal constraints, but it cannot alone define what makes a life good. That requires the complementary inquiry of Ikigaiology — and the voices of the people affected.
